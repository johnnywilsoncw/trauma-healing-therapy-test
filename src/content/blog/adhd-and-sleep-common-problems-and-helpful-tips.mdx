It’s 1 a.m., your body is worn out, and your brain has just decided to replay a conversation from three years ago.

Sleep trouble and ADHD show up together often, for teens and adults alike. The same brain that struggles to pause during the day can struggle to power down at night, and a short night makes focus, mood, and patience harder the next day. That loop is exhausting, and it isn’t a character flaw.

## Where Sleep Tends to Go Sideways

A racing mind is the most familiar problem. Once the distractions of the day fall away, thoughts get louder, and your attention jumps from worry to plan to random memory without any permission from you.

Timing is another. Many people with ADHD symptoms feel wired late at night and foggy in the morning, which makes an ordinary bedtime feel almost impossible. Teens are often hit hardest, since early school start times collide with a body clock that already runs late. Time blindness adds to it, because “one more episode” or “one more email” turns into 2 a.m. before you notice.

Then there’s bedtime procrastination. After a day of meeting everyone else’s demands, the late hours can feel like the only time that belongs to you, so you hold on to them even though you’ll pay for it tomorrow.

## Small Changes That Make a Real Difference

Start with a wind-down your brain can follow without much effort. Pick two or three steps, such as dimming the lights, putting your phone across the room, and taking a warm shower, then do them in the same order every night. Repetition does the work that willpower can’t. Keep the steps small enough to manage even on a rough day, because a routine that feels like a chore is one you’ll drop by Thursday.

Give your thoughts somewhere to go before bed. Keep a notepad by your pillow and write down tomorrow’s to-do list, the worry that keeps circling, and the idea you’re afraid of losing. Getting it out of your head and onto paper often quiets the loop enough to let you drift off.

Set an alarm for bedtime, not just for waking up. Because time slips away from you, a reminder that says “start winding down now” works better than hoping you’ll notice the hour. Bright light in the morning and dimmer light in the evening can also nudge a late body clock earlier. Caffeine in the afternoon and, for some people, the timing of ADHD medication can affect sleep too, so bring that up with your prescriber rather than guessing.

## When the Tips Aren’t Enough

Sometimes you do everything on the list and still lie awake. It’s worth asking a doctor about medical causes such as sleep apnea. Often the trouble also points to something deeper keeping your nervous system on alert, whether it’s anxiety, a lifetime of feeling behind, shame about what didn’t get done, or old stress that never got the attention it needed. No amount of sleep hygiene can reach any of those on its own.

Do you have to untangle every reason before your nights improve? No. But working on the core issues underneath, rather than stacking more rules on top, tends to be what finally shifts things. Talk therapy gives you room to look at the patterns behind them at your own pace.

You also don’t have to fix this alone or all at once. Sleep is one of those areas where small, steady changes add up, and where kindness toward yourself matters more than a perfect routine.
